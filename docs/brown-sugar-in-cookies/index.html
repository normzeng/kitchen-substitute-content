Substitute for Brown Sugar in Cookies

Brown sugar is where cookie chew comes from. One of these swaps is chemically identical; the others trade the chew away.

If you have molasses, this one's not even a substitution: brown sugar is white sugar plus molasses, manufactured. 1 cup white + 1 Tbsp molasses per cup (2 Tbsp for dark) behaves identically in dough.

The swaps

  • Brown sugar
    • White sugar + molasses — per 1 cup brown sugar — 1 cup white sugar + 1 Tbsp molasses (2 Tbsp for dark) — 200 g + 20-40 g
      What changes in cookies: nothing — chemically identical; work the molasses through with your fingers or a fork
    • White sugar — per 1 cup brown sugar — 1 cup white sugar — 200 g
      What changes in cookies: crisper, paler, less chew — molasses is the moisture and acidity that keeps cookies soft
    • Coconut sugar — per 1 cup brown sugar — 1 cup packed coconut sugar — 200 g
      What changes in cookies: slightly less sweet, darker bake, similar chew
    • Muscovado sugar — per 1 cup brown sugar — 1 cup, packed — 213 g
      What changes in cookies: deeper, more intense molasses flavor

Cookie questions

Why did my all-white-sugar cookies come out crisp?
Brown sugar is acidic and hygroscopic — it grabs moisture and keeps grabbing it after baking. White sugar dries out and sets crisp. If chew matters and you have no molasses, underbake by a minute instead.
Does the molasses swap work in creaming?
Yes — cream the molasses in with the butter and sugar together. The acidity that brown sugar would contribute is right there in the molasses.
